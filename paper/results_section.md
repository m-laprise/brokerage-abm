# Results

## 5.1 The broker primarily provides assessment, not access

Across the simulations, the broker is the dominant channel in the market. Principals route most demand to the broker rather than to self-search: the outsourcing rate, the share of match demand sent to the broker, is 0.93 at baseline, rising within a run from 0.81 early to 0.93 late, and it stays high across the no-capture regimes. Principals prefer the broker because its matches are better. The realized value of broker-channel matches exceeds that of self-search matches in nearly all regimes, with an output gap (broker minus self) averaging +0.42 across no-capture regimes. This is the model's rendering of why relational work gets outsourced: the broker constructs better matches than clients do on their own.

What allows the broker to construct better matches is an advantage in assessment, not access. The access fraction, the share of brokered matches between two agents who could not already reach each other through their network, is low, with a late-window mean of 0.16 across no-capture regimes, and it declines over time within every run. Most brokered matches connect principals who already knew each other; the broker's contribution is to identify which of them pair well, not to provide reach (Fig. 3).

The broker's assessment advantage lies in better ranking, not in precise valuation. On a held-out sample of random partners (Appendix A §8), the broker orders prospective pairings well (rank correlation 0.89 with the true ordering at baseline, and 0.91 to 0.95 on the easiest problems) but predicts output levels poorly (holdout R2 of 0.65 at baseline, and negative on the hardest problems). Principals are weak on both, with a holdout rank correlation of 0.51 and a holdout R2 of −2.6. Ranking is what matchmaking requires, so what the broker's services rely on is a good ordering of candidate pairings, more than an accurate appraisal of their absolute worth.

Within the model, the broker acts not as an appraiser of general quality, but as a matchmaker who knows which parties suit each other. Its value comes from the ranking of complementarities, and it holds even where clients can reach counterparties directly.

## 5.2 How the matching problem shapes the broker's advantages

Recall that ρ sets the direction of the matching problem, from pure general quality to pure complementarity, and that the gain δ sets its difficulty through the effective rank of the output (see model description above). Interestingly, across regimes, the broker's structural position tracks ρ while its prediction edge tracks δ, and the two need not move together.

The structural measures vary with ρ and are flat in δ. Broker betweenness, the share of shortest paths between agents that run through the broker, is higher where matching is more complementarity-driven (0.82 at ρ = 0 against 0.12 at ρ = 1). The access fraction, the share of brokered matches that connect previously unconnected pairs, moves the opposite way (0.12 against 0.24). Under complementarity (ρ = 0), the broker is more central but, in practice, bridges less: a larger share of its matches re-pair agents already within reach of each other.

Meanwhile, the prediction measures vary with δ. Ordering is what the broker needs, and its rank correlation with realized output stays high across regimes (0.91 to 0.95 on the simplest problems, 0.89 at baseline, lower on the most complex; Fig. 2), well above the agents' throughout (0.51 at baseline). Performance at predicting the output behaves differently: the broker's holdout R² at ρ = 0 is +0.85 when the output is simple but strongly negative when it is complex (−2.4 at δ = 0.5, −3.9 at δ = 0.75), worse than predicting the average and, on the hardest complementarity problems, worse than the agents' own, despite the broker seeing both sides of every match and pooling across the market. Ranking which match is better thus withstands difficulty far better than predicting how good it will be, and the broker's ordering edge, though it narrows on the hardest complementarity problems, is what persists.

Realized match quality follows neither measure cleanly: the broker still produces better matches than self-search wherever matching is relational, most of all where it is also complex (the output gap is +0.73 at ρ = 0, δ = 0.5, against +0.12 at ρ = 1; Fig. 1), consistent with the idea that the broker matters most where match value turns on complementarity. One pattern is unexplained, however. The broker's realized output advantage is largest exactly where its ranking advantage over agents is smallest, in the hard complementarity regimes: at ρ = 0 the rank-correlation gap is 0.32 at δ = 0 and 0.11 at δ = 0.75. Why this is remains an open question.

### 5.3 Opportunity, behavior, and the production of structural advantage

In almost all regimes, the broker's structural position strengthens over time and the agent network thins. At baseline, mean agent degree falls (27.1 to 23.1) and the broker's betweenness centrality rises (0.60 to 0.73), and this co-movement holds in 86 of 91 no-capture regimes (Fig. 3). Turnover removes ties about as fast as they appear, and the network does not densify. This raises a crucial question: why is the structural advantage not self-liquidating [even at low turnover--verify]?

The answer comes from the fact that structural position is endogenous and produced by the dynamics of a matching market. It depends on which agents form ties and which agents go to the broker. A brokered match adds a new agent-to-agent tie (after which the broker no longer lies on their shortest path) only when the two were not already connected (Appendix A §5b, §8). Hence, the broker's work densifies the network only when it provides access, not when it provides assessment. And across regimes, access is rare. The five exceptions are the pure-quality regimes, in which agents easily predict quality from attributes and match well on their own, such that outsourcing rates are lower [VERIFY] and over time, the network densifies [VERIFY] and the broker's centrality erodes. Identifying regimes in which brokered *access* dominates remains an open question.

Across no-capture regimes, the broker's structural advantage, measured by betweenness centrality, is negatively correlated with the access fraction (−0.66): the broker is most central where it bridges least. In other words, bridging opportunity and bridging behavior move in opposite direction. This is another counter-intuitive finding in light of existing theory. Clients value the broker's assessment services, and outsource to it in large numbers, thus increasing its centrality. Conversely, its advantageous position does not cause the broker to provide more access matches.

Because access is low throughout (a late mean of 0.16, and declining within every no-capture run), the densifying effect is weak and the broker's structural advantage persists. Where brokering is assessment rather than access, self-liquidation of the structural advantage need not arise.

Realized output tracks betweenness centrality, not access fraction or prediction quality: across no-capture regimes, the output gap is positively correlated with betweenness (+0.82) and negatively with the access fraction (−0.46) and the broker's holdout ranking (−0.66) (Fig. 4). These co-variations across regimes are not an effect of structural position: complementarity-dominated regimes are sparse and brokered-assessment-heavy, with the highest betweenness centrality and the largest output gaps [VERIFY]. Quality-dominated regimes are self-search focused, with low centrality and smaller output gaps [VERIFY]. ### 5.4 Conditions and consequences of capture

In the capture variant, the broker may stop working on behalf of a client and instead buy that client's demand and place it as a principal (Appendix A §9). How much it does so varies with the features that shape its advantage, and with the risk it must bear.

Capture by the broker is most extensive where matching is complimentarity-dominated: the captured share of outsourced demand is 0.97, 0.96, and 0.93 at ρ = 0, 0.3, and 0.5, and lower toward the quality end (0.77 and 0.76 at ρ = 0.7 and 1). The sharpest control for capture rates is the reservation threshold f_r, which sets the inventory risk the broker takes on (see model description): the captured share is 1.00, 0.93, 0.78, and 0.20 at f_r = 0.4, 0.6, 0.9, and 1.2 (Fig. 5). Capture rates are largely insensitive to problem difficulty (0.99 at δ = 0 against 0.96 at δ = 0.75) and to the pace of agent turnover [RESULTS?FIGURE?].

Capture is therefore most extensive in regimes in which the broker's assessment advantage is largest and the risk of acting as principal is smallest; structural position does not appear to shape capture.

Capture reorganizes the market (Fig. 6). Because a principal placement connects the broker to each side but creates no tie between the two clients, the agent network thins out: at baseline, mean agent degree under capture is about half its no-capture level (10.5 against 23.1), and matches per agent are also lower and decreasing. The broker's own betweenness is slightly higher (0.78 against 0.73). The access fraction reverses, high and rising within runs under capture (about 0.83 against 0.16 without capture), because in a thinned network the broker is increasingly the only path between agents. The self-liquidating densification of ordinary brokerage, in which each match erodes the gaps the broker bridges, is suspended.

Outsourcing is also lower under capture (a late mean of 0.70 against 0.90), because of the interaction between the pricing and decision rules. A captured client is paid its own historical average realized value (Appendix A §9). Since standard brokered matches realize above-average value, clients tend to be less satisfied on average with being bought out by the broker than they are with concluding a brokered match. Over time, in the capture baseline [VERIFY], satisfaction with the broker channel trends downward, returning some demand to self-search.

Captured transactions freeze the clients out of forming new ties, and this lock-in produces a thinning network, as described above. By the same token, capture removes learning opportunities: principal placements are recorded only in the broker's history, so agents learn from fewer matches under capture than without it (roughly 2,100 against 4,800 observations per period, with about 55% of matches under capture writing nothing to agents [what are these numbers? baseline? late means?]).

Yet, on the measures of prediction performance on a holdout set, the agents perform no worse [than what? than the broker? than without capture?]. Their ranking is essentially unchanged (0.51 in both cases [which cases?]) and their level calibration is better (holdout R2 of −1.2 against −2.6). One reading, which is not tested here, is that without capture, when outsourcing is high, the clients' out-of-sample weakness comes mainly from learning on a selected sample, the broker's top-scored pairings. Capture reduces the outsourcing rate and makes the sample they observe less selected. A fuller account of client learning, with and without capture, is deferred to future work.
